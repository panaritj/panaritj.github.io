The Gatekeeping of Jargon

May 28, 2026 · 2 min

Complex terminology often acts as an unintentional gatekeeper. In disciplines like machine learning and data analytics, the nomenclature can sound incredibly intimidating. Terms like “stochastic gradient descent” or “multi-head attention” sound almost mystical to an outsider. Yet, when you break them down, the core concepts, taking small steps down a hill to find the lowest point or weighing which words in a sentence relate most to each other, are intuitive. Idempotence sounds scary, but saying that pressing the elevator button 10 times for it to come down is the same as pressing it once, sounds easy. Memoization sounds harder than saying, “I write an answer on a sticky note so I do not have to do the math again.”

You are gambling away your learning opportunity if you let the jargon gatekeep you from exploring a concept. One mental trick I have learned after all these years is to avoid judging the difficulty of a concept by its name.

Compression vs. Comprehension#

Terminology exists to quickly convey information to experts in a specific field by compressing information. They are a useful shorthand for experts to communicate quickly. When we are just learning and aren’t experts yet, this dense language naturally scares us off. Describing an entire phenomenon in a single word is also inherently difficult, which often makes these terms overly meta. Throw in a little Latin, add a dash of wanting to sound fancy, and voilà: you end up with an often unnecessarily complex, cluttered label that obscures a clean and simple idea.

Go back to the term again#

Now, and this is the important part: once you grasp a concept, take a moment to go back and reason out why its name was chosen. It turns out that the term often captures the absolute heart of the concept, and understanding why that specific name was selected is a great way to test your comprehension. After you learn about idempotency, looking up its Latin roots, Idem (same) and Potens (powerful), and translating it to “same effect” makes everything click into place.
