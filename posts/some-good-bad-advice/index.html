Some good bad advice

May 28, 2026 · 7 min

Between watching the new Spider-Noir and upsetting myself with the June 7th election drama in my country, I opted for a third option: writing this article. Truth be told, looking at the political landscape back home, it’s hard not to get some popcorn, shut off your brain, put on the hat of a football fan, and watch some fruitless debates. I almost regret not capitalizing on those heavily subsidized diaspora flights just to go back and vote; there really is nothing quite like the irony of people deciding the fate of a country without having to bear any of the daily consequences.

Enough derailing. Some good recommendations first. Please be wary of the advice you accept, including the advice you find here. Everyone’s circumstances vary: different personalities, different educational backgrounds, different locations, and different opportunities. Usually, the best advice is tailored to your situation. Be honest with yourself, recognize your circumstances, and do not get worn down over something that is outside of your control. I am writing this article more as a reminder to myself about my own situation. Moreover, people tend to generalize something that worked or did not work for them into bulletproof advice that they then preach relentlessly.

This article will give you advice that I consider generally bad. Some of the examples are geared more toward software engineers, or as I like to call them, the new Baustelle. That is just because it comes easier and more naturally to me; nevertheless, the advice should be digestible for everyone. Some of this advice is stuff I have done or am doing, which I recognize to be bad and want to improve upon.

This article is a “Buffet of Bad Advice.” The points are not necessarily linked; simply pick and master what you need.

The Buffet#

Visit Your Parents Rarely#

The less, the better. Honestly, this paragraph does not fit well with the rest of the points, but who cares? I had to say it; it hurts. Seeing them once or twice a year is more than enough, and make sure it is no more than a weekend per visit. God forbid you live with them or call them every day. They do not get you and your world; they still have that old mentality that is now long gone, and their life experience is now irrelevant. They are not allowed to make mistakes and they cannot learn. Your friends are the cool ones, the ones that really understand you and the world, and the ones who will be there for you when no one else is.

I was speaking with a working student a couple of years back. A handsome guy (not as much as me, of course), 19-20 years old or so, a good student, smart, driven, and just doing well in life. He was really concerned with his situation; he said, and I am paraphrasing here, that he had to go live with his parents for financial reasons, and was worried that he would have a hard time finding a girlfriend due to living with his parents. This situation really hit me; it’s one of those problems that (I think mostly Western) society creates in young people that shouldn’t even exist. His struggle was real, though. People nowadays really consider not living with your parents as a hallmark of success, and in a relationship, it is a big no-no. At the end of the day, true success is paying 60% of your income to share a windowless kitchen with three strangers just to prove your independence so you do not have to stay in the spacious house of your parents who live in the same city.

Worship the Status Quo#

Whine and complain a lot, to the point where you do the bare minimum. This is very important. Also, try to group up with similar people and colleagues whenever possible. Open a Slack channel specifically for this; this is where you will be spending most of your day. It is never your fault. Things cannot be made better, and nothing is under your control. This is also a perfect way to lose opportunities; a new project is coming up in your team, but you refuse to be part of it because there will be a lot of inefficiencies and you just cannot stand it. You must live in the status quo. That workshop organized to discuss what can be improved is useless; no one cares anyway. That CI problem that has existed forever and those tests that are flaky are not worth fixing. Proposing an idea, going through all the PR comments, having it checked by your team, doing a PoC, speaking with the PM to prioritize it, maybe raising it higher up the chain for a better chance of getting it on your team’s agenda, having calls with various people, trying to convince them, pitching the idea to non-technical people … it is all just too much.

Avoid All Socialization#

Do not socialize. You have begun your studies or work. Now is the time where you must grind 24/7. That meetup, that weekend trip with friends, or that after-work event is too time-consuming. That feeling that you might be losing time should be fully embraced. At the end of the day, it FULLY boils down to being competent. There is always LinkedIn where you can apply for your next job, and because you are the best, you will get it. You also do not need emotional connections; you are strong, fully independent, and can deal with everything on your own. Oh, and when you do decide to socialize, make sure that alcohol is involved. If possible, go to a nightclub and get drunk.

Doubt Yourself Constantly#

Doubt yourself constantly. By default, everyone is smarter and more capable than you. Always put people on a pedestal, especially. When opportunities come, let them slip because you are not good enough. You also cannot get better; you just do not have it in you.

Eradicate Joy#

Eradicate joy. Have no fun at work. If someone posts a meme in the team channel, reply with a link to the company’s code of conduct. This is how one-third of your life must go. You go to work, be super serious, and never crack a joke. When someone does and you want to laugh, make sure you hold back your smile.

Always read for the sake of finding mistakes#

If you find one, you are automatically smarter than the person who wrote the thing you read. Reading for understanding, extracting knowledge, having a back-and-forth discussion, or sparring over ideas are all secondary. Oh, and in the end, make sure to nullify the entire thing you read just because you found a single mistake. This, though, is not enough; you must project this mistake onto the person’s overall capabilities.

Weaponize the Pull Request#

It is either your way or no way. There is only one correct way of doing things, and that is your way. Of course, make sure to mask it under the umbrella of “keeping standards high.” If a PR uses a variable name that is totally fine but not exactly what you would have written, block the PR and make sure you tell them you are “guarding the codebase consistency.” Give a junior engineer a topic and make them the “owner.” Let them spend two weeks researching, prototyping, and building it. Then, during the final review, swoop in and say, “Yeah, I see what you did here, but we actually need to do it this way instead.”

Reject All New Technology#

Always be the pessimist. Discard every new technology that comes up, by default, automatically. Find a thread of possibility of failure and hold on to it for dear life; make it your main argument, your hill to die on. You know those people who post once a week on LinkedIn showing how an LLM could not answer an apparently simple question? Use that as fuel, and then stop using LLMs altogether.
